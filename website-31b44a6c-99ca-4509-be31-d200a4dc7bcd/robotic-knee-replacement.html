The Procedure

What is robotic knee replacement?

Robotic knee replacement is not surgery performed by a robot. Your surgeon remains in control throughout. The robotic system is a precision instrument that helps them plan and execute the operation more accurately than is possible with conventional manual instruments alone.

Before surgery, imaging of your knee is used to build a three-dimensional model of your anatomy. Your surgeon uses this model to decide exactly where the implant should sit, how much bone to remove, and how the ligaments should be balanced — all before making a single incision.

During the operation, the robotic arm provides real-time feedback and helps keep the bone preparation within the planned boundaries, protecting the surrounding soft tissue.

Conventional vs. Robotic

Conventional: implant position is judged using mechanical jigs and the surgeon's experience, with alignment confirmed largely by eye.

Robotic-assisted: implant position is planned digitally in 3D, then executed with continuous feedback — improving consistency of alignment and soft-tissue balance.

Better alignment matters because an implant that sits correctly tends to feel more natural and is subjected to more even wear over the years.

Am I A Candidate?

Who is suitable for knee replacement?

You may benefit if you have:

  • Persistent knee pain that limits walking or climbing stairs
  • Advanced osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis of the knee
  • Pain at night or at rest that disturbs sleep
  • Progressive bow-leg or knock-knee deformity
  • Little or no relief from medication, injections or physiotherapy

Surgery may be deferred if:

  • Your symptoms are still well controlled without surgery
  • An active infection is present and needs treating first
  • Other medical conditions need to be stabilised before an operation
  • Weight reduction or targeted physiotherapy may give meaningful relief

Only a clinical examination with appropriate imaging can determine whether joint replacement is appropriate for you. We will always discuss non-surgical options first where they are realistic.

Recovery

Your recovery timeline

Day 0–1

Most patients stand and take supported steps within hours of surgery, guided by a physiotherapist.

Week 1–2

Walking with a stick or walker, wound review, and daily range-of-motion exercises at home.

Week 3–6

Increasing independence, stair climbing, and progressive strengthening of the thigh muscles.

Month 3+

Return to routine activities, light exercise and travel, with periodic follow-up reviews.

This timeline is a general guide. Your actual recovery depends on your age, general health, the condition of the joint before surgery, and how consistently you follow the rehabilitation programme.

FAQ

Common questions

Is the surgery performed by a robot?

No. Your surgeon performs the operation and remains in control at every stage. The robotic system is an assistive tool that improves planning accuracy and provides real-time guidance during bone preparation.

How long does the surgery take?

A single knee replacement typically takes between one and two hours, though this varies depending on the complexity of the deformity and whether one or both knees are being treated.

How long will I stay in hospital?

Most patients stay two to four days. Your surgeon will confirm the expected duration based on your general health and how quickly you mobilise.

Is robotic surgery more painful?

Generally the opposite. Because the approach is minimally invasive and disturbs less soft tissue, most patients report less pain in the early post-operative period compared with conventional surgery.

How long does a knee implant last?

Modern implants commonly last well over fifteen years. Accurate alignment — one of the main advantages of robotic assistance — helps reduce uneven wear that can shorten implant life.

Can both knees be done at the same time?

In selected patients, yes. Whether to operate on both knees in one sitting depends on your age, cardiac and respiratory fitness, and your surgeon's assessment of the risks involved.

Will I be able to sit cross-legged or squat?

Many patients regain a good range of movement, but deep squatting and floor-sitting are not guaranteed after knee replacement. Your surgeon will give you a realistic expectation for your specific case.
